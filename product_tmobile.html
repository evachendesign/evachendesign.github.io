T-Mobile

Account dashboard redesign

The T-Mobile Account Dashboard is a central hub that empowers customers to manage their accounts efficiently. It encompasses essential features such as bill payments, device management, plan adjustments, and family line controls. Given its complexity and significance, the dashboard's information architecture is crucial for ensuring users can navigate and utilize these services effectively. The redesign aimed to enhance user self-service capabilities, thereby reducing reliance on customer support and contributing to operational cost savings.

define problems

From business challenge to UX problems

I reframed a costly call-center issue into a UX challenge: redesigning the account dashboard to improve self-service and reduce support calls.

Business challenge

Customer support centers handle millions of calls every month, creating significant operational costs.

Research insights
  • Customer experience team: Over 30% of calls come from users asking for help with tasks that should be available via self-service online.
  • UX research: Both the website and app suffer from poor navigation and low information discoverability, preventing users from completing tasks independently.
Project scope

Focus on redesigning the Account Dashboard, a critical entry point for users managing billing, usage, plans, and devices.

Design goal

Improve information discovery and scannability so users can quickly find features and self-serve online without contacting support.

Success metric

Target a 20% reduction in support calls related to account management.

process 1

Improving findability through data-driven information architecture redesign

Research methods

Card sorting • Usability testing • Data analysis

Part 1: Translating user mental models into business-ready information architecture

From card sorting to information architecture design

After Product and I listed the features for Account Management, we partnered with UX Researchers to run card sorting. While the research revealed users' mental models for organizing account features, the final IA required adjustments to balance user expectations with operational constraints, here is why:

  • Team ownership boundaries: T-Mobile's organizational structure assigns specific features to dedicated teams (Usage, Payment, Account). The adjusted IA respects these boundaries while maintaining intuitive user pathways.
  • Functional logic V.S. user perception: Users categorized "Settings" independently, though functionally these features belong under Account management. We restructured to align with system architecture while preserving discoverability.
  • Business promotion: While users expected benefits/discounts exclusively in Shop (confirmed through tree testing), high customer dissatisfaction with findability demanded dual placement—maintaining Shop as primary while adding contextual entry points within Account features.

Part 2: Validation

Prototype for findability testing

Based on synthesized card sorting results, business requirements, and data analysis, I developed the adjusted IA structure into a mid-fidelity prototype for findability testing.

Testing results & iterations

Overall findability scored 6.2 out of 7, a strong result. Based on insights, we made targeted refinements.

Iteration 1 – Benefits visibility
  • Finding: Users did not expect to find benefits under the Plan Details page and requested more upfront visibility.
  • Iteration: Despite Baymard’s caution against promotional clutter, I added a Benefits card on the Account page to improve discoverability while keeping the design lightweight.
Iteration 2 – Check if our customizations to the information architecture are acceptable to users?
  • Finding: Users accepted Device and Settings under Account, but did not find Usage and Payment when moved to the top navigation. Many continued to look for them within Account. This issue was amplified on mobile, where the navigation bar is hidden behind a hamburger menu.
  • Iteration: I restored Bill and Usage to the Account and Device sections to align with user expectations and improve visibility across devices.

Part 3: Data-driven information architecture design

Multi-source insight gathering

To inform detailed IA decisions, I synthesized insights from three critical stakeholder groups:

  • Customer experience team: Analyzed recurring Customer Care call topics to identify navigation pain points.

  • Product analytics: Examined traffic patterns and conversion metrics to refine the page hierarchy.

The convergence of these data sources revealed overlapping problem areas, validating information priorities and ensuring alignment across teams.

process 2

Responsive web visual design

Part 1: Mobile view first

Why I pick mobile view design first and include it in the previous findability testing? Because:

  • Majority of the T-Mobile users use mobile. (>70%)
  • The natural of the account dashboard is a bank of portal and help people navigate to many features. The main thing I want to consider first is the information hierarchy. The mobile straight scrolling down nature, helped me avoid many noises, and focused on considering information hierarchy.

My visual design goal: Scannability. Help users find their task as quickly as possible.

First exploration

Visual exploration of three layouts for the account page: card, list, and tabbed. Card view was chosen for its superior scannability.

Fidelity evolution

The mobile account dashboard progressed from low-fidelity sketches to mid-fidelity wireframes and finally to high-fidelity mockups.

Part 2: From mobile to desktop

My goal for desktop was efficiency. While mobile relies on vertical scrolling, desktop should leverage horizontal space for faster scanning. I used the device details page for early exploration because it is the most information-dense and the hardest to optimize for scanability. If this page worked, it could set the composition standard for the rest of the site.


Two-column layout was selected as the best balance of scanability and hierarchy.

Part 3: Edge cases and design quality assurance

Before handoff, I conducted edge case and design quality assurance to ensure the account dashboard performs reliably under real-world conditions.


I validated how each card adapts to various data states—such as full, empty, loading, and error—while maintaining hierarchy, consistency, and visual balance across devices.


Working closely with engineers, I defined behavior rules and fallback logic for every card, testing for long text, high data volume, and missing data scenarios. This process confirmed that the design system remains scalable and resilient under all conditions.
